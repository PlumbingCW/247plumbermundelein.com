Hot Water Recirculating Pump for a Tankless Water Heater in Mundelein, IL

A tankless heater makes hot water fast, but the pipe between it and your tap still holds cold water. A recirculating pump shortens that wait.

Call (847) 281-8626

By the Mundelein 24/7 Plumbing team. Published November 12, 2026. Updated November 12, 2026.

Why the wait happens

Hot water has to travel from the heater to the fixture. The pipe is full of cooled water from the last use, and that water has to leave before hot arrives. The longer the pipe and the larger its diameter, the longer the wait and the more water goes down the drain.

A tankless heater does not change that. It makes hot water quickly, but it cannot move the pipe's cold slug closer to your sink.

What a recirculating pump does

A pump moves water through a loop so hot water stays near the fixtures. In a dedicated return line system, a return pipe brings water back to the heater. In a comfort system without a return, the pump uses the cold line as a temporary return and fixes the problem at a lower install cost.

Either way the pump runs on a timer, a temperature sensor or a button. Running it only when needed matters because a constant loop wastes energy and shortens equipment life.

Three common setups

SetupHow it worksTypical tradeoff
Dedicated return lineSeparate pipe back to the heaterBest performance, more pipe work
Crossover valve under the farthest fixtureUses the cold line as the returnSimple install, warm cold water for a while
Built-in recirculation on the heaterInternal pump and small bufferNeeds compatible model and loop

Which tankless models support recirculation

Many modern tankless units list a recirculation mode, and some include a small internal buffer. Others expect an external pump. Check the manual or ask the plumber to confirm before buying the pump, because a mismatched pump can trip the heater's flow sensor.

If you are planning a new tankless install, ask for the recirculation plan in the same quote so the pipe and controls are laid out once.

Cost, noise and energy

The pump itself is the smaller expense. Labor, the return line and any electrical outlet near the heater add to the total. A good pump is quiet, but a poorly mounted one can hum through a floor joist.

Energy depends on how often the loop runs and how well the pipe is insulated. Insulating the hot line is cheap and cuts heat loss, so add it during the job.

Retrofit versus new construction

In a new build, a dedicated return line is easy because the walls are open. In an existing house, the plumber must fish a return pipe or use the crossover approach. The retrofit approach costs less and works well on short runs, and the dedicated return performs best on long ones.

Be honest about how often the far fixture is used. A guest bathroom used twice a month does not justify the same loop as a master suite used daily.

Pump failures and what they sound like

Recirculation pumps are small and run often. A failing one may whine, rattle or run hot to the touch. The pump may also seize after sitting unused. Replacement is usually quick if the unit was installed with isolation valves on both sides.

Ask for those valves in the quote. They make the next service visit shorter and cheaper.

Energy and safety checks

Recirculation loops raise the temperature of water that sits in pipes. Set the heater so delivered water is safe at the tap, and consider a thermostatic mixing valve if children live in the house. The pump should shut off when the loop is hot so it does not run for no reason.

These are small details that make a large difference in daily comfort and running cost.

Troubleshooting a pump that does not help

If hot water is still slow, check that the pump is running and that the check valve is oriented the right way. A pump fighting the wrong flow direction does nothing useful. Also confirm the timer matches your schedule, since a pump set for the wrong hours will be off when you need it.

A plumber can test the loop temperature at the far fixture in minutes.

Codes and standards

Recirculation loops should follow the heater maker's manual and the Illinois Plumbing Code, the state standard (Title 77, Part 890 of the Illinois Administrative Code) for pipe, valves and backflow protection.

When to call a plumber about a recirculation loop

Most pump problems are annoying, but a few are safety issues.

  • Scalding water at the tap means the heater setting or the mixing valve needs attention.
  • A pump that runs hot, smells like burning or trips a breaker should be switched off.
  • A leak at the pump or a valve should be stopped with the nearest shutoff.
  • Error codes on the tankless unit after a pump is added call for a service visit.

Questions to ask before buying

  • Does my heater model support a recirculation pump and loop?
  • Will you use a timer, a sensor or a push button?
  • How will the pump be mounted to avoid noise?
  • Is the hot line insulated, and is that in the quote?
  • Does the price include an outlet or power connection?

When recirculation is not worth it

A short run to a single bathroom may not need a pump. Moving a fixture closer to the heater or adding a small point-of-use unit can solve the same problem for less. Tell the plumber which fixtures bother you most.
