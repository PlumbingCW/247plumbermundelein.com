Running Toilet Repair Cost in Mundelein, IL: What to Expect

Most running toilets need a flapper or a fill valve. The part is cheap. The cost is in diagnosing it and the water you waste while you wait.

Call (847) 281-8626

By the Mundelein 24/7 Plumbing team. Published November 12, 2026. Updated November 12, 2026.

Why a running toilet matters

A toilet that runs can waste a large amount of water in a month, and every gallon goes through your sewer bill too. It also wears down parts and can mask a bigger leak. The cost of waiting is higher than the cost of fixing.

Often, the cause is a flapper that no longer seals or a fill valve that no longer shuts off.

What a plumber visit includes

A visit covers diagnosis, parts and labor. Most plumbers carry common flapper and fill valve sizes, so the repair is done on the first trip. The price reflects the call, the time and the warranty, not just the part.

Ask whether the price includes a replacement of the supply line and shutoff valve if they are old. Those are common add-ons that avoid a second leak.

What you can do yourself

Adjusting a float and swapping a flapper are common do-it-yourself fixes. Shut off the supply, flush to empty the tank, remove the old flapper, and install a matching one. Test with food coloring.

If the water level will not hold, the toilet still runs, or the supply valve is stuck, stop and call. Forcing an old valve can create a bigger leak.

How to avoid repeat repairs

Use a quality flapper that matches your toilet model. Avoid tank cleaning tablets that sit in the tank, since chlorine breaks down rubber. Check the tank every year for mineral build-up and corroded parts.

A fill valve and flapper that are both original are likely to fail close together, so replacing both saves a second call.

A quick home test sequence

First, lift the lid and see whether water is spilling into the overflow tube. Second, add food coloring and wait ten minutes to test the flapper. Third, lift the chain and see if the flapper closes freely. Fourth, press the flapper down by hand. If the running stops, the flapper or chain is the cause.

If none of these change the sound, the fill valve is the likely culprit. A plumber can confirm in a few minutes.

Why some toilets keep failing

Toilets with hard-water scale on the flush valve seat, old rubber that has gone soft or a chain that snags on the lid all fail repeatedly. A replacement flapper that does not match the toilet model also leaks soon after install. Matching the part to the brand makes the repair last.

If you have replaced the flapper twice, ask the plumber to check the seat for pitting.

Saving water in the long run

Toilets are the largest indoor water user in many homes. A running toilet adds to that, and old models use much more water per flush than current ones. If yours is from the early 1990s or older and keeps failing, a new efficient model will cut your water use and your bill.

Ask for a high-quality fill valve and flapper kit either way, since cheap parts fail sooner.

When a leak is not the toilet at all

If the sound of running water continues after you have checked the tank, look elsewhere. A leaking supply line to the toilet, a dripping faucet nearby or a failing valve at the water heater can mimic a running toilet. Shutting the toilet's supply valve and listening tells you quickly whether it is the source.

If the noise stays, a plumber can trace it with a listening device.

What a visit does not include

A repair visit fixes the toilet tank. It does not fix a clogged drain, a cracked bowl or a leaking floor flange, which are separate jobs. If the toilet also drains slowly or rocks, tell the dispatcher so the plumber brings the right tools.

A clear description at the start keeps the visit short and the price fair.

If the running sound is not the toilet

A hiss that continues after the tank is ruled out can come from a leaking line elsewhere in the house, which leak detection traces without opening walls needlessly.

Codes and standards

Toilet replacement parts and fittings fall under the Illinois Plumbing Code, the state standard (Title 77, Part 890 of the Illinois Administrative Code), and a replacement toilet must meet current water use limits.

Questions to ask the plumber

  • Which part is failing and why?
  • Is the quote for one part or the whole flush assembly?
  • Will you replace the supply line and shutoff while you are there?
  • What warranty comes with the repair?
  • Is there any sign of a bigger leak?

Repair or replace the toilet

A toilet from the early 1990s may use much more water per flush than a current model. If it also has a cracked bowl or a wobbly base, replacement can be the smarter use of money. See toilet repair for what we check.
